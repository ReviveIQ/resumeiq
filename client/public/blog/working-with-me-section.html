What Is a Working With Me Section on a Resume?

Every resume answers what you've done. Almost none answer who you are to work with. That's the question the Working With Me section exists to answer — and it's the one hiring managers are asking every time they read your work history.

By Bryan Greer · June 30, 2026 · 7 min read

The question every hiring manager is asking — but can't answer from your resume

There's a moment in every hiring process that most candidates never see. A hiring manager has narrowed the field to two or three finalists. The resumes are similar. The experience is comparable. The credentials check out.

The question they're trying to answer isn't "can this person do the job?" — they know the answer to that. The question is: what is it actually like to work with this person?

How do they communicate when something goes wrong? Do they prefer to process information quickly or deliberately? Will they clash with the existing team dynamic? Do they need detailed direction or do they push forward independently? Are they conflict-avoiders who will quietly agree in meetings and relitigate decisions later?

A resume of titles, companies, and bullet points can't answer any of these questions. Every candidate looks like a high performer on paper. That's the problem the Working With Me section solves.

The core insight

Your resume proves what you've done. The Working With Me section tells them who they're hiring. Those are different documents answering different questions — and only one of them exists on most resumes.

What exactly is a Working With Me section?

A Working With Me section is a professional behavioral summary, typically 200–350 words, appended to your resume after your experience, skills, and education. It tells hiring managers and future colleagues how you actually operate — not your job duties, but your work style, your defaults under pressure, your communication preferences, and the conditions that bring out your best work.

It is not a personality essay. It is not a list of soft skills ("collaborative," "detail-oriented," "team player"). It is a structured, evidence-based behavioral profile derived from validated personality assessments — the same kind of data organizations pay for through tools like Predictive Index, DiSC, or MBTI — translated into professional language a hiring manager can act on.

Unlike every other section of your resume, it doesn't describe your past. It describes your future — how you'll show up, how you'll operate, what your manager can expect from day one.

What does a Working With Me section include?

A well-constructed Working With Me section covers five dimensions:

The 5 dimensions of a Working With Me section
Communication Style
How you prefer to give and receive information. Direct vs. diplomatic. Verbal vs. written. Frequent check-ins vs. async updates. What you need to feel informed without being micromanaged.
Decision Making
Whether you move quickly on available data or prefer to gather more before committing. How you handle ambiguity. Whether you are consensus-driven or willing to make the call alone.
Collaboration
How you work in a team dynamic. Whether you naturally lead, support, challenge, or coordinate. What you need from others to do your best work.
Under Pressure
How you respond when deadlines compress, priorities shift, or conflict arises. Whether you tend toward urgency or steadiness. What stress looks like from the outside — and how to work with you through it.
What Brings Out Your Best
The environment, leadership style, and team dynamic where you consistently perform at the highest level. What you need from a manager and a culture to fully engage.

Where does it come from?

The most credible Working With Me sections are grounded in validated assessment data rather than self-description. Anyone can write "I'm a strong communicator who thrives in fast-paced environments." That's noise. A profile derived from actual assessment results — showing a high-D, low-C DiSC style, or an ISTJ Myers-Briggs type, or a Predictive Index Strategist pattern — is signal.

The most commonly used assessments as source material are:

  • DiSC — Dominance, Influence, Steadiness, Conscientiousness. Covers behavioral tendencies in work contexts.
  • Myers-Briggs (MBTI) — 16 personality types across four dimensions: Extraversion/Introversion, Sensing/Intuition, Thinking/Feeling, Judging/Perceiving.
  • Predictive Index (PI) — Four factors (Dominance, Extraversion, Patience, Formality) used heavily in enterprise hiring contexts.
  • Thomas-Kilmann (TKI) — Five conflict-handling modes: Competing, Collaborating, Compromising, Avoiding, Accommodating. Particularly useful for leadership roles.
  • 360-degree feedback reports — Patterns from peers, managers, and direct reports, if available.

When multiple assessments are available, they can be synthesized into a single coherent profile — the areas of agreement across assessments are the most reliable signals about how you actually operate.

Important

A Working With Me section should never mention the name of the assessment it was derived from. "According to my DiSC profile..." is awkward and unnecessary. The value is in the behavioral language, not the source citation.

A real example

Here's what a Working With Me section looks like in practice, for a senior sales leader with a Strategist PI profile, high-C DiSC tendencies, and ISTJ Myers-Briggs typing:

Example — Senior Account Executive

Communication Style. Prefers direct, substantive communication with clear context and stated intent. Works best with written briefs over verbal-only discussions for complex decisions. Provides consistent, structured updates without requiring prompting — and expects the same from collaborators.

Decision Making. Deliberate under ambiguity, thorough before committing, and confident once decided. Moves quickly when the data is sufficient; slows down when it's not — and is vocal about the distinction. Doesn't reverse decisions without cause, and holds others to the same standard.

Collaboration. Operates as an independent contributor who integrates well with high-accountability teams. Respects expertise without deferring unnecessarily. Gives direct feedback, receives it without defensiveness, and values colleagues who do the same.

Under Pressure. Default response to urgency is to systematize — break the problem into components, assign ownership, set timelines. Stabilizing force in a crisis rather than a reactive one. Can appear reserved in high-emotion situations; this is focus, not disengagement.

What Brings Out My Best. High-autonomy environments with clear accountability, strong individual contributors around me, and a manager who provides direction and then trusts execution. Thrives in roles where the definition of success is clear and performance is measured directly against it.

Why it matters more now than it did five years ago

Three things have changed in hiring that make the Working With Me section more valuable now than it's ever been:

1. More resume volume, less time per resume. The average hiring manager spends 6–8 seconds on an initial resume scan. The candidates who make it to the next round aren't necessarily the most qualified — they're the ones who stopped the scroll. A behavioral profile at the bottom of a strong resume is a pattern interrupt that most hiring managers will actually read, because it's different from everything else they've seen that day.

2. Remote and hybrid work made cultural fit harder to evaluate. When you're hiring someone who will primarily communicate through Slack and Zoom rather than in-person, the question of "how do they actually operate" becomes more consequential — and harder to assess. A Working With Me section gives hiring managers and future teammates a window into that operating system before a single interview.

3. Personality assessments are now standard in enterprise hiring. Most companies with over 200 employees are already using some form of behavioral assessment in their hiring process. Bringing your own data — synthesized into professional language — signals self-awareness, preparation, and genuine investment in finding a mutual fit rather than just getting an offer.

What it is not

A few things worth being explicit about:

  • It is not a soft skills list. Writing "collaborative, detail-oriented, and a strong communicator" tells a hiring manager nothing. Every candidate claims these. The Working With Me section is specific, behavioral, and grounded in evidence.
  • It is not a cover letter summary. It doesn't restate your experience or explain why you want the job. It describes how you operate.
  • It is not a replacement for the professional summary. The summary opens the resume and establishes your career identity. The Working With Me section closes it and adds behavioral context. Both have a role.
  • It is not appropriate for every application. For roles where speed and volume are the primary dynamic (entry-level, high-turnover roles), a behavioral profile adds length without proportionate return. It earns its place most clearly in mid-to-senior individual contributor and leadership roles, especially where team dynamics, stakeholder management, or cultural fit are explicit evaluation criteria.

How to add one to your resume

There are two ways to build a Working With Me section:

Option 1 — Write it yourself. Pull your assessment reports, identify the consistent themes across them (the patterns that show up in two or three different assessments are the most reliable), and write behavioral descriptions in each of the five dimensions. Have someone who has worked closely with you read it and tell you whether it sounds accurate. Revise until it does.

The bottom line

Two candidates with identical work histories are not identical candidates. The difference between them isn't on the resume — it's in how they communicate, how they make decisions, how they handle conflict, what kind of management they need, and what environment brings out their best work.

Most hiring managers discover this in the first 30 days after someone starts. The Working With Me section lets them discover it before they make the offer.

Your resume tells them what you've done. The Working With Me section tells them who they're hiring. Only one of those is differentiated.
